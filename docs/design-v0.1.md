# Little Ages

## v0.1 — First Settlement

**Status:** Product design baseline  
**Repository:** `joewolly/LittleAges`  
**Primary host:** Always-on Windows PC  
**Primary clients:** Web browser on Windows, macOS, iPhone/iPad, or another LAN device  
**Backend:** C# / .NET 10 LTS / ASP.NET Core  
**Persistence:** SQLite  
**Frontend:** React + TypeScript  
**World renderer:** PixiJS or equivalent high-performance 2D renderer

This document is the preserved v0.1 product-design baseline. It records the
original product intent, scope, non-goals, and release criteria; it is not a

## 1. Product Vision

**Little Ages is a persistent autonomous civilization simulation that creates its own factual history.**

The player does not directly control individual citizens. Instead, Little Ages creates a world, populates it with simulated people, establishes a small set of physical and social rules, and allows history to emerge from the interaction of those systems.

The central fantasy is:

> Start with a handful of people, leave them alone, and return later to discover what happened.

Citizens should form relationships, specialize, gather resources, build homes, create families, age, die, and leave consequences that affect later generations.

Little Ages is not primarily a traditional city builder. It is a **living historical simulation that happens to be observable like a miniature world**.

Long term, the player should be able to watch a civilization for hundreds or thousands of simulated years and investigate why it became what it became.

## 2. Core Product Principles

### 2.1 Simulation creates reality

The authoritative truth of Little Ages comes from simulation state.

An LLM must never decide whether:

- somebody is hungry;
- somebody builds a house;
- two people become friends;
- somebody dies;
- resources run out;
- a settlement prospers;
- an invention occurs;
- a conflict begins.

Those events must be generated by simulation rules.

AI may eventually interpret, summarize, narrate, or query those events, but it may not create canonical historical facts that did not occur in the simulation.

### 2.2 Stories must emerge rather than be scripted

Little Ages should produce stories because systems interact.

Example:

- food becomes scarce;
- a particularly industrious citizen spends more time gathering;
- that citizen becomes highly skilled;
- others benefit from the increased food supply;
- repeated successful interactions improve relationships;
- a household forms;
- descendants inherit some characteristics;
- the family eventually becomes unusually influential.

There should not be code saying:

> Create an influential family after 30 years.

The rules should merely make such an outcome possible.

### 2.3 Every important outcome should be explainable

The simulation should preserve enough causal information to answer:

- Why did this citizen die?
- Why did these people become friends?
- Why did population decline?
- Who built this structure?
- Why did the settlement run out of food?
- Which family has been here the longest?
- What happened during Year 37?
- Why is this person considered important?

Little Ages should favor **inspectability over opaque complexity**.

### 2.4 History is a first-class system

Historical data is not debug logging.

Important events must be stored as structured, queryable simulation facts.

The eventual AI historian, newspaper, historical search, family trees, statistics, biographies, and civilization summaries should all derive from the same event history.

### 2.5 Persistence is part of the fantasy

The world should feel like it exists independently of the UI.

Closing the browser must not stop the civilization.

The Windows server owns the world.

The browser observes it.

### 2.6 Determinism matters

Given:

- the same world seed;
- the same configuration;
- the same simulation version;
- the same player interventions;

Little Ages should produce the same outcome.

Simulation speed must not alter history.

Running at 1× and running at maximum speed must produce equivalent canonical results.

## 3. v0.1 Product Goal

The purpose of v0.1 is not to build a complete civilization simulator.

The purpose is to prove that the smallest useful set of systems can create an interesting autonomous settlement.

### The v0.1 question

> Can 20 simulated people create a settlement whose first 100 years are genuinely interesting to inspect?

If the answer is yes, Little Ages has a viable foundation.

---

## 4. v0.1 Definition of Success

A successful unattended 100-year simulation should contain:

- multiple generations;
- births and deaths;
- distinct family lines;
- friendships and rivalries;
- changing occupations or specialties;
- construction;
- periods of scarcity and prosperity;
- citizens with visibly different life histories;
- resource trends;
- population changes;
- identifiable consequential events;
- an accurate historical timeline explaining major developments.

The simulation does **not** need wars, governments, currency, religion, or advanced technology to pass v0.1.

The test is whether the existing systems interact deeply enough to create stories on their own.

## 7. Simulation Architecture

Little Ages should use an **authoritative single-writer simulation engine**.

Only the simulation engine may mutate canonical world state.

The web client, database layer, APIs, metrics system, and eventual AI systems observe or request actions through defined interfaces.

Conceptually:

```text
                    ┌───────────────────────┐
                    │      Web Client       │
                    │ React + TypeScript    │
                    └───────────┬───────────┘
                                │
                      REST + WebSocket
                                │
                    ┌───────────▼───────────┐
                    │    ASP.NET Server     │
                    │                       │
                    │ API / Query Layer     │
                    └───────────┬───────────┘
                                │
                    ┌───────────▼───────────┐
                    │ Simulation Runtime    │
                    │                       │
                    │ Single authoritative │
                    │ state writer         │
                    └───────────┬───────────┘
                                │
              ┌─────────────────┼─────────────────┐
              ▼                 ▼                 ▼
       Citizen Systems      World Systems      History
              │                 │                 │
              └─────────────────┼─────────────────┘
                                ▼
                         SQLite Database
```

Parallelization may later be introduced for calculations that cannot change simulation ordering.

v0.1 must prioritize deterministic results over maximum CPU utilization.

## 8. Simulation Time

Canonical time is represented as an **integer number of simulated minutes since world creation**.

Floating-point timestamps must not be authoritative.

Example:

```text
WorldMinute = 19,452,830
```

Calendar values are derived from this number.

### Calendar

For v0.1:

- 24 hours/day;
- 7 days/week for display purposes;
- 30 days/month;
- 12 months/year;
- 360 days/year;
- four 90-day seasons.

A simplified 360-day calendar makes simulation calculations easier while remaining intuitive.

---

## 9. Discrete-Event Simulation

Little Ages should not execute every citizen's full decision system every rendered frame.

Instead, the simulation uses scheduled events.

Example:

```text
08:00   Elara begins gathering wood
08:00   Tomas begins gathering food
08:10   Mira wakes
08:20   Mira chooses breakfast
09:05   Tomas completes gathering
09:15   Elara completes gathering
```

Each citizen maintains:

```text
CurrentAction
ActionStartedAt
ActionCompletesAt
NextDecisionAt
```

When nothing meaningful occurs between simulation minutes, fast-forward mode may advance directly to the next scheduled event.

This is essential for long historical runs.

---

## 10. Deterministic Event Ordering

Events occurring at the same simulation minute must be processed using a stable ordering rule.

Minimum ordering key:

```text
SimulationMinute
EventPriority
EntityID
EventSequence
```

No result may depend on:

- thread scheduling;
- database timing;
- network timing;
- frame rate;
- WebSocket timing;
- UI refresh rate.

---

## 11. Randomness

Each world receives an immutable 64-bit world seed.

Randomness must be deterministic.

Random systems should use separate reproducible streams or derived seeds for domains such as:

```text
WorldGeneration
CitizenGeneration
DecisionVariation
Relationships
Reproduction
Mortality
ResourceRegeneration
```

The implementation must prevent unrelated changes in one subsystem from unnecessarily changing random outcomes in another subsystem.

A single global `Random` instance shared throughout the simulation is prohibited.

## 12. World Model

### Default map

Initial target:

```text
160 × 160 tiles
25,600 tiles
```

This is large enough to create meaningful geography without creating unnecessary simulation cost.

Map size should be configurable for testing.

### Tile data

Each tile contains immutable or slowly changing properties such as:

```text
Coordinate
Elevation
TerrainType
Walkability
Fertility
WaterAccess
ResourceNodes
MovementCost
```

### Initial terrain types

- freshwater;
- grassland;
- forest;
- rocky ground;
- dense wilderness.

Terrain generation must be seed-based.

The initial settlement should be placed in a survivable area with reasonable access to:

- water;
- food;
- wood;
- stone.

The world generator must reject seeds whose starting conditions are obviously nonviable.

---

## 13. Resources

v0.1 uses only three canonical material resources:

```text
Food
Wood
Stone
```

Water access is an environmental requirement rather than an inventory resource.

### Food

Food should be renewable but constrained.

Sources can abstractly represent:

- foraging;
- fishing;
- naturally available food.

Food availability varies by season.

### Wood

Trees provide wood.

Forest resources regenerate slowly.

### Stone

Stone is finite or regenerates negligibly during v0.1 timescales.

### Resource location matters

Citizens must physically travel to resource locations.

As nearby resources are depleted, travel cost should increase naturally.

This creates an important emergent pressure without requiring artificial difficulty modifiers.

---

## 14. Seasons

Seasons affect the world without implementing detailed weather.

Initial effects:

### Spring

- high food regeneration;
- normal movement;
- favorable birth/resource conditions.

### Summer

- highest food availability;
- normal movement.

### Autumn

- moderate food availability;
- preparation pressure emerges naturally.

### Winter

- low food regeneration;
- increased energy needs;
- greater shelter importance.

No scripted "winter crisis" exists.

A winter crisis should occur only if settlement conditions cause one.

Canonical citizen data should include at minimum:

```text

DeathCause?

HomeStructureID?
Location
Health
Traits
Skills

```

Age is derived from `BirthMinute`.

Dead citizens remain permanently queryable.

They must never simply disappear from the database.

## 16. Citizen Lifecycle

Life stages are derived rather than manually assigned.

Suggested baseline:

```text
0–5       Young child
6–12      Child
13–17     Adolescent
18–59     Adult
60+       Elder
```

Children have limited productivity.

Adults perform the majority of settlement work.

Elders may remain productive with gradually reduced physical efficiency.

There should not be a deterministic "death age."

Mortality is probabilistic and influenced by:

- age;
- health;
- starvation;
- prolonged unmet needs;
- severe exposure.

The age curve should make unusually long lives possible but uncommon.

---

## 17. Needs

v0.1 citizens maintain four primary needs:

```text
Hunger
Rest
Shelter
Social
```

Health is a state rather than a need.

Each need ranges from:

```text
0.0 = completely satisfied
1.0 = critical
```

Needs change analytically according to elapsed simulation time rather than requiring constant per-minute updates.

Example:

```text
CurrentHunger =
    HungerAtLastUpdate
    + HungerRate × MinutesElapsed
```

Values are evaluated when relevant.

This allows fast simulation without millions of redundant updates.

---

## 18. Personality

Citizens receive continuous personality traits between `0.0` and `1.0`.

Initial traits:

```text
Industriousness
Sociability
Curiosity
Cooperativeness
RiskTolerance
Resilience
```

Traits should influence probabilities and utility scoring rather than act as hard behavior switches.

A citizen with high industriousness should generally work more often but should still eat when starving.

A highly social citizen should seek interaction more often but should not abandon survival needs.

---

## 19. Skills

Initial skills:

```text
Foraging
Woodcutting
Stoneworking
Construction
Hauling
Domestic
```

Citizens improve skills by performing relevant work.

Skill affects:

- action duration;
- resource yield;
- construction efficiency;
- action success where applicable.

Skills should use continuous experience internally.

UI-friendly skill levels may be derived from experience.

---

## 20. Occupations

v0.1 should **not assign rigid jobs**.

Occupation is a derived description of what someone actually does.

Example:

If a citizen spends most productive time collecting wood and becomes highly skilled at it, the UI may describe that citizen as:

> Lumberjack

If their behavior changes over several years, their occupation may change.

Suggested derived labels:

- Forager;
- Lumberjack;
- Stoneworker;
- Builder;
- Hauler;
- Generalist.

This creates specialization without implementing a labor-management UI.

---

## 21. Decision System

Citizen behavior uses utility-based decision making.

At a decision point, a citizen generates currently possible actions.

Example:

```text
Eat
Sleep
SeekShelter
GatherFood
GatherWood
GatherStone
Build
Haul
Socialize
Rest
Idle
```

Each candidate receives a score.

Conceptually:

```text
Utility =
    NeedUrgency
  × PersonalityModifier
  × SkillModifier
  × SettlementDemand
  × ContextModifier
  × RelationshipModifier
  - TravelCost
  - ActionCost
  + DeterministicVariation
```

The highest viable action is selected.

Exact weights belong in configurable simulation tuning data rather than scattered constants.

---

## 22. Decision Explainability

Every selected citizen action should be capable of reporting the major factors that caused it.

Example internal explanation:

```text
Action: GatherFood

Food shortage             +0.42
Personal hunger           +0.21
Foraging skill            +0.16
Nearby food source        +0.09
Industriousness           +0.08
Travel cost               -0.05

Final utility              0.91
```

This does not need to be permanently stored for every decision.

However, debug/developer tooling should be able to expose it.

This will be invaluable when emergent behavior looks wrong.

---

## 23. Relationships

Relationships are persistent pairwise social state.

For each meaningful citizen pair:

```text
Familiarity
Affinity
Trust
Conflict
LastInteraction
InteractionCount
```

Values evolve through actual interactions.

Examples:

Working together successfully may increase:

- familiarity;
- trust.

Positive social interactions may increase:

- affinity.

Resource competition or repeated negative interactions may increase:

- conflict.

Relationship labels are derived:

```text
Stranger
Acquaintance
Friend
Close Friend
Rival
Partner
Family
```

The labels do not drive relationships.

Underlying values do.

---

## 24. Partnerships and Families

Eligible adults may form partnerships when conditions align.

Factors can include:

- mutual affinity;
- familiarity;
- age;
- existing partnership state;
- household stability;
- personality;
- deterministic random variation.

Partnership formation must create a historical event.

Partnerships may theoretically dissolve later, but complex separation behavior is not required for initial v0.1.

---

## 25. Children

Children may be born to compatible partnered citizens.

Birth probability should depend on:

- adulthood;
- partnership;
- household shelter;
- settlement food security;
- existing children;
- recent births;
- population pressure.

This prevents population growth from being entirely disconnected from settlement conditions.

Children inherit a mix of:

- parental personality tendencies;
- bounded random variation.

Skills are **not inherited directly**.

Family relationships and ancestry must remain queryable indefinitely.

---

## 26. Households

A household represents citizens sharing domestic life.

A household may contain:

- partners;
- children;
- dependent relatives.

Households have:

```text
HouseholdID
MemberIDs
DwellingID
CreatedAt
DissolvedAt?
```

v0.1 does not implement household wealth.

The settlement primarily uses shared resources.

---

## 27. Settlement Economy

The first settlement operates primarily as a cooperative resource pool.

This intentionally avoids premature market simulation.

Settlement state includes:

```text
FoodStored
WoodStored
StoneStored
StorageCapacity
ShelterCapacity
Population
```

Citizens consume food from shared storage.

Gathered resources generally enter shared storage.

Buildings consume shared resources.

Future versions may replace this with private property, trade, markets, taxes, or class systems.

---

## 28. Construction

v0.1 building types:

### Shelter

Purpose:

- housing;
- sleep efficiency;
- winter protection.

### Stockpile

Purpose:

- increases resource storage capacity.

### Workshop

Purpose:

- improves selected construction or material-working efficiency.

Each structure records:

```text
StructureID
Type
Location
ConstructionStartedAt
CompletedAt?
BuilderContribution
ResourceCost
Occupants
Condition
```

Structures must be physically placed in the world.

---

## 29. Construction Demand

Buildings should not be created because a scripted timeline says they should.

Construction requests emerge from settlement conditions.

Examples:

```text
ShelterCapacity < Population
→ shelter demand increases

Storage nearly full
→ stockpile demand increases

Population / construction volume increases
→ workshop becomes useful
```

Builders then choose construction activity through the normal decision system.

---

## 30. Movement

Citizens physically occupy world positions.

Pathfinding should use the tile grid.

A* or an equivalent deterministic pathfinder is sufficient for v0.1.

Movement cost may vary by terrain.

Path caching is encouraged.

Rendering interpolation must not become authoritative simulation state.

The server owns logical location and movement.

The client makes it look smooth.

---

## 31. Structured Memory

Citizens have limited persistent memories.

Memories are not free-form text.

Example:

```text
Memory
{
    EventID
    Type
    SubjectCitizenID?
    Location?
    Importance
    EmotionalValence
    CreatedAt
}
```

Examples of memorable events:

- birth of child;
- death of partner;
- formation of friendship;
- severe conflict;
- completion of home;
- starvation period;
- major settlement milestone.

Memory may influence later social decisions.

Citizens should retain only a bounded number of lower-level memories while critical life events remain permanently available through historical records.

## 34. Event Importance

Events receive an importance level.

Suggested scale:

```text
0 Debug
1 Routine
2 Personal
3 Notable
4 Major
5 Historic
```

Only levels 2–5 normally appear in the user-facing timeline.

This allows Little Ages to distinguish:

> Tomas became friends with Mira.

from:

> The settlement lost 31% of its population during the Winter Famine.

## 37. Persistence

SQLite is the authoritative persistent store for v0.1.

Recommended configuration:

- WAL mode;
- foreign keys enabled;
- versioned migrations;
- transactional state writes;
- explicit indexes for historical queries.

Simulation memory may contain the active working state, but enough information must be persisted to safely recover the world.

---

## 38. Save Strategy

Little Ages should implement:

### Periodic checkpoint

Persist canonical simulation state on a configurable schedule.

Suggested default:

```text
Every 6 simulated hours
```

during normal-speed operation.

Fast-forward mode may batch checkpoints based on real time to avoid excessive disk traffic.

### Important-event checkpoint

Critical lifecycle events may trigger persistence boundaries when appropriate.

### Graceful shutdown

Windows service shutdown must save canonical state.

### Crash recovery

A crash may lose a small bounded amount of simulation time, but the database must remain valid.

---

## 39. Save/Reload Determinism Requirement

This must be explicitly tested.

Two simulations begin with identical configuration.

### Run A

```text
Year 0 → Year 100 uninterrupted
```

### Run B

```text
Year 0 → Year 37
save
shutdown
restart
Year 37 → Year 100
```

Canonical Year 100 state must match.

At minimum compare:

- citizens;
- deaths;
- births;
- relationships;
- structures;
- resources;
- world state;
- historical events.

## 44. Visual Direction

v0.1 does not require final art.

However, it should establish the eventual visual identity.

The desired feeling is:

> **A living miniature historical diorama.**

Not:

> developer dashboard with circles moving around.

Temporary procedural graphics are acceptable, but the rendering architecture should support later replacement with:

- illustrated terrain;
- animated citizens;
- seasonal visuals;
- structures;
- roads;
- environmental detail.

The name **Little Ages** should influence the eventual design language: warm, miniature, historical, observational, and slightly storybook-like.

---

## 45. Simulation Speeds

Initial controls:

```text
Paused
1×
10×
100×
1,000×
MAX
```

Recommended semantic baseline:

```text
1× ≈ 10 simulated minutes per real second
```

The exact visual rate may be tuned.

`MAX` is different.

MAX mode prioritizes simulation throughput over animation and may advance directly between scheduled events.

At high speeds:

- individual animations may be suppressed;
- WebSocket update frequency should be throttled;
- UI receives summarized state;
- simulation results must remain identical.

---

## 46. Headless Mode

The backend must be capable of simulation without the web UI.

Example future CLI interface:

```text
littleages simulate \
    --seed 12345 \
    --years 100 \
    --max-speed
```

This is critical for:

- automated testing;
- balance testing;
- reproducibility;
- performance benchmarking;
- running thousands of simulated civilizations later.

## 47. Performance Targets

v0.1 should be designed around:

```text
Starting population:       20
Expected normal range:     20–500
Target supported range:    1,000
Operational safety limit:  configurable
Map:                       160 × 160
Historical duration:       100+ years
```

The normal UI should remain responsive with hundreds of citizens.

MAX headless mode should be able to simulate the 100-year acceptance world in practical minutes rather than hours on the target Windows machine.

Exact performance gates should be established after the first benchmark build rather than prematurely optimizing arbitrary numbers.

---

## 48. Population Safety

Because an emergent reproduction model can accidentally create exponential growth, an operational safeguard is permitted.

Example configuration:

```text
Simulation.MaxPopulation = 2000
```

This must be treated as a simulation rule and stored with the world configuration.

Approaching the limit should reduce birth eligibility before reaching a hard failure.

The eventual goal is for resource constraints and household conditions to regulate population naturally.

---

## 49. Reliability

The server must tolerate:

- browser disconnects;
- browser refreshes;
- no connected clients;
- Windows sleep/resume where possible;
- clean service restart;
- unexpected process termination.

A lost web client must have zero effect on canonical simulation progression.

---

## 50. Observability

Development builds should expose:

- current world minute;
- event queue size;
- citizen count;
- simulation events/second;
- simulation years/real minute;
- DB checkpoint duration;
- active action counts;
- decision evaluation counts.

Optional debug inspection should allow examining why a citizen chose a specific action.

This information should not become a player-facing analytics dashboard unless it serves the game.

---

## 51. Testing Strategy

Little Ages requires more than conventional unit testing because systemic interactions matter.

### Unit tests

Cover:

- calendar calculations;
- need decay;
- utility scoring;
- relationship changes;
- inheritance;
- mortality curves;
- resource regeneration;
- construction requirements;
- event ordering.

### Determinism tests

Same:

```text
seed + config + version
```

must produce identical results.

### Save/reload tests

Interrupted execution must match continuous execution.

### Invariant tests

Examples:

- dead citizens cannot act;
- resources cannot become negative;
- children cannot have impossible birth dates;
- a citizen cannot be their own ancestor;
- structure occupancy cannot exceed hard capacity;
- citizen IDs never collide;
- historical events reference valid entities;
- simulation clock never moves backward.

### Long-run simulation tests

Automated runs:

```text
1 year
10 years
100 years
500 years
```

should check for:

- crashes;
- NaN values;
- runaway population;
- permanent starvation loops;
- deadlocks;
- broken ancestry;
- unbounded event growth;
- impossible resources.

### Statistical tests

Across many seeds, verify that the simulation does not always produce effectively identical civilizations.

Variation is required.

---

## 52. The 100-Year Acceptance Test

This is the primary v0.1 release gate.

### Starting conditions

```text
Seed: fixed acceptance seed
Population: 20
Settlement: 1
Player interventions: none
Duration: 100 simulated years
```

### Required outcome

The simulation completes successfully and produces:

- surviving or historically explainable extinct population;
- multiple generations when conditions permit;
- family trees;
- structures;
- changing resource levels;
- relationship histories;
- births;
- deaths;
- specialization;
- historical events;
- citizen biographies;
- settlement statistics.

Extinction is not automatically a test failure.

If extinction occurred because the simulated conditions legitimately caused it, and the event history clearly explains why, the simulation behaved correctly.

A mysterious extinction caused by broken AI or resource logic is a failure.

---

## 53. The Fun Test

Automated correctness is not enough.

After the 100-year run, somebody should be able to inspect the world and find meaningful questions worth investigating.

Examples:

> Who had the most children?

> Who lived the longest?

> Which citizen contributed the most construction work?

> Which family persisted the longest?

> When was food scarcity worst?

> Who were the closest friends?

> Did anyone become rivals?

> Which winter killed the most people?

> What is the oldest surviving building?

> Which citizen's life changed the settlement the most?

If the answers are technically present but nobody cares enough to ask the questions, the simulation needs more depth.

## 58. AI Boundary

No external model provider is required for v0.1.

The architecture should expose read-only historical APIs suitable for a future AI historian.

Future AI receives:

```text
structured events
citizen records
family relationships
settlement statistics
historical aggregates
```

AI may answer:

> Why did the settlement decline in Year 83?

But the correct workflow is:

```text
query canonical history
↓
retrieve evidence
↓
generate explanation
```

Never:

```text
ask model what probably happened
↓
invent history
```

## 64. Foundational Rule for Future Development

Every proposed feature should answer:

## 65. Final v0.1 Product Statement

**Little Ages: First Settlement** begins with twenty people in an untouched world.

They are not characters following scripts.

They are simulated individuals with:

- needs;
- personalities;
- skills;
- relationships;
- families;
- histories.

They gather what they need.

They choose how to spend their time.

They build.

They form attachments.

They raise children.

They grow old.

They die.

The settlement changes because they lived there.

Little Ages records what actually happened and allows the player to watch, inspect, and eventually understand that history.

The first release succeeds when we can start with twenty strangers, simulate one hundred years without intervention, and come back to a world whose history feels worth reading.
